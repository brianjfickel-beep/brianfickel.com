Mapping the Organizational Terrain

The org chart shows who’s supposed to have power. This shows who actually does. Map the people inside your organization who can move, shape or quietly stop your effort, then decide who to see first and who should carry the message.

Use roles or initials, not names, and never enter classified, CUI or sensitive information. This page has no server and sends nothing anywhere. Your map is saved in this browser only if you ask it to be.

Why the org chart isn’t the map

Every organization has two structures. The formal one is on the org chart: who reports to whom and who signs what. The informal one is how work actually gets done: who people go to for advice, who they trust, and who quietly shapes a decision before it reaches the boss. New advisors tend to work the chart. Experienced ones work the terrain. This tool helps you map it, using three well-established ideas.

1. Power has more than one source

Social psychologists John French and Bertram Raven identified the bases of power in 1959, and Raven later added information. A person can hold several at once, and someone with little formal authority can be strong on the others.

2. Influence runs through informal networks

David Krackhardt and Jeffrey Hanson showed that informal networks, especially the people others go to for advice and the people they trust, often explain how an organization really behaves better than its formal chart does. Watch who drafts the first version, who people check with before they commit, and who can slow a decision without ever saying no. Those people may be two levels below the decision-maker, and they may be the most important stop you make.

3. Stance tells you how to engage

Grant Savage and colleagues paired each stakeholder’s potential to help with their potential to threaten. The strategy follows from where someone sits: involve supporters, collaborate with those who could go either way, defend against opponents, and monitor everyone else. The map below uses the same logic. Powerful supporters become your messengers. Powerful skeptics get an early, private conversation before any group meeting.

How to rate

  • Rate for this effort. Someone central on budget may not matter on a communication question.
  • Rate what you’ve seen, not what the title implies. If you don’t know, say so; unknowns are a finding.
  • A quiet veto is anyone who can slow or stop the effort without formally deciding: the person who controls the calendar, the staffing, the legal review or the boss’s ear.
  • Use roles or initials. This is a working map for you, not a document to circulate.
